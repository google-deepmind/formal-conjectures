In abstract algebra , Jacobson's conjecture is an open problem in ring theory concerning the intersection of powers of the Jacobson radical of a Noetherian ring . It has only been proven for special types of Noetherian rings, so far. Examples exist to show that the conjecture can fail when the ring is not Noetherian on a side, so it is absolutely necessary for the ring to be two-sided Noetherian. The conjecture is named for the algebraist Nathan Jacobson who posed the first version of the conjecture. Statement [ edit ] For a ring R with Jacobson radical J , the nonnegative powers J n {\displaystyle J^{n}} are defined by using the product of ideals . Jacobson's conjecture: In a right-and-left Noetherian ring , ⋂ n ∈ N J n = { 0 } . {\displaystyle \bigcap _{n\in \mathbb {N} }J^{n}=\{0\}.} In other words: "The only element of a Noetherian ring in all powers of J is 0." The original conjecture posed by Jacobson in 1956 [ 1 ] asked about noncommutative one-sided Noetherian rings, however Israel Nathan Herstein produced a counterexample in 1965, [ 2 ] and soon afterwards, Arun Vinayak Jategaonkar produced a different example which was a left principal ideal domain . [ 3 ] From that point on, the conjecture was reformulated to require two-sided Noetherian rings.
